Problems I discovered from connecting your ideas
11. The city treats symptoms rather than the whole water system

A flood, polluted river, clogged drain, groundwater decline, etc. may be treated as separate problems even though they are physically connected.

12. Departmental fragmentation

Water, drainage, roads, sewage, irrigation and environmental interventions can be planned separately, creating coordination problems.

13. Repeated road digging

A road can be repaired and then opened again for another utility project because infrastructure work is not sufficiently coordinated.

14. Poor intervention timing

A technically good project can still be a bad decision if it is done immediately before another planned project.

15. Desilting can become reactive rather than preventive

The city removes sediment after a problem appears without adequately understanding:

where sediment comes from
why it accumulates
how quickly it will return
16. Desilting itself may create another problem

River sediment may contain sewage-related contaminants or heavy metals, so there is a “what do we do with the removed sediment?” problem.

17. “River cleaning” is often confused with “river restoration”

Removing silt or trash does not necessarily solve:

sewage entry
polluted runoff
erosion
encroachment
ecological degradation
18. Lack of traceability for pollution

It's easy to say a river is polluted; it's harder to identify:

which outlet/source caused the deterioration and by how much.

19. Lack of spatial prioritization

A city may know many places have problems but still struggle to answer:

Which location should we fix first?

20. Lack of consequence-based prioritization

The most physically damaged location isn't necessarily the most important one.

A smaller drainage problem affecting 100,000 people may deserve priority over a larger problem affecting 5,000.

21. Lack of “what-if” capability

Cities often analyze the existing situation, but the more powerful question is:

What happens if we intervene here instead of there?

22. Infrastructure decisions aren't always linked to citizen impact

Technical decisions don't naturally translate into:

people affected
road closure duration
flood reduction
service improvement
disruption avoided
23. Lack of a single water balance

Water exists in many forms and systems:

rainfall
drains
rivers
lakes
groundwater
drinking water
irrigation
wastewater

It is difficult to see the whole movement of water as one system.

24. Groundwater recharge isn't necessarily placed where it is most useful

You can have places with abundant runoff but poor recharge conditions, or permeable areas where runoff isn't effectively captured.

25. Water quality and water quantity are often considered separately

Having water available doesn't necessarily mean it is usable for:

irrigation
ecological purposes
groundwater recharge
other uses.
26. Flood problems are not only “too much rain”

They can result from:

insufficient drain capacity
blockages
sedimentation
poor connectivity
urbanization
loss of storage
altered flow paths
27. Citizen-generated information is underused

Residents often know:

where flooding happens
how long water stands
where sewage smells/enters
where roads are repeatedly dug

But this local knowledge isn't necessarily connected to formal infrastructure data.
