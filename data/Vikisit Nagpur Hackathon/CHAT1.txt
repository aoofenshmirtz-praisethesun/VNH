Weighed performance optimization strategies balancing visual impact with low-end hardware constraints
Yes, and the trick is that most of what reads as "dazzling" is cheap. The expensive stuff is usually the part nobody notices.

Where the cost actually is

Heavy Three.js sites are slow because of a small number of specific things: real-time shadow maps, postprocessing stacks (bloom, SSAO, DOF), many lights, high polygon counts, large uncompressed textures, and rendering at full retina resolution. Remove those and WebGL runs fine on Intel integrated graphics.

What's nearly free: a single fullscreen quad with a fragment shader. Gradient meshes, flowing noise, aurora effects, animated grain — that's one draw call. At 1x resolution an integrated GPU handles it at 60fps without noticing. Most of the "wow, this site is beautiful" shader sites you've seen are exactly this, not actual 3D scenes.

A potato-safe Three.js budget

Under ~50k triangles total, aggressive instancing, few draw calls
MeshBasicMaterial or matcaps instead of PBR; bake your lighting into the texture
Zero real-time shadows — a blurred blob texture on a plane looks better anyway
Textures ≤1024px, compressed to KTX2/Basis
antialias: false, and clamp renderer.setPixelRatio(Math.min(devicePixelRatio, 1.5)) — drop to 1.0 or even 0.75 and upscale on weak hardware
No postprocessing pass unless you fake it inside your main shader
Render on demand: only call render() when something actually changed, and pause via IntersectionObserver + visibilitychange
The part that matters most for a government context

Assume WebGL might not work at all. Locked-down government machines run old Chromium builds, sometimes with GPU blacklists that silently fall back to software rendering (SwiftShader), which is far worse than no 3D. So:

Try to get a WebGL2 context. If it fails, or if detect-gpu reports a low tier, never initialize the scene.
Fall back to a static hero image or a pure-CSS gradient animation. Design the fallback deliberately so it still looks intentional, not broken.
Respect prefers-reduced-motion — this is often a hard accessibility requirement in government procurement, not a nicety.
The cheaper aesthetic route

Honestly, for a hackathon demo, consider skipping 3D entirely. Layered CSS gradients, a Canvas 2D particle field, transform/opacity-only scroll animations (these run on the compositor thread and cost almost nothing), and genuinely good typography and spacing will get you 80% of the visual impact for 5% of the performance cost. Judges respond to coherence more than to polygon count.

Test it honestly

Chrome DevTools → Performance → CPU throttling 6x, plus the FPS meter under Rendering. Also run Lighthouse. If your site can't hit 30fps under 6x throttling, it will stutter on a 2018 office desktop.

What you build: NagpurWatch
Four pieces, in priority order. Ship 1 and 2 or you have nothing; 3 and 4 are what win.

1. The map. Every surveillance device in Nagpur as a pin — CCTV, ANPR, facial recognition. Each pin carries: operator (NMC / Nagpur Police / NSSCDCL / private), legal authority it was installed under, the tender or GR it came from, retention period, and whether that field is known or unknown. Unknown is the important state. Most pins will be grey.

2. Crowdsourced submission. Anyone photographs a camera, drops a pin, tags the type. Feeds an OSM-compatible schema (man_made=surveillance, surveillance:type, operator, camera:direction) so your data isn't trapped in a hackathon demo — it can flow upstream into OpenStreetMap. Say that on stage; it signals you built something with a life after Sunday.

3. The transparency ledger. Cross-reference pins against public tender documents from NSSCDCL and NMC. Two columns: cameras procured on paper vs cameras verified on the ground. Every discrepancy is a story. This is the part no other team will have.

4. RTI generator. Pick any grey pin, and the app generates a filled, filing-ready RTI application under the Right to Information Act 2005 — correct PIO, correct department, correct 30-day clock — asking who operates it and what the retention policy is. One click from "I see a camera" to "I have filed for an answer." This is your closing demo moment and it is genuinely excellent.

Stack
Leaflet plus OpenStreetMap tiles, a Postgres/PostGIS or plain SQLite backend, and a mobile-web capture page. No 3D, no heavy ML. Everything you told me about potato PCs applies here: this must run on a municipal desktop and a ₹7,000 phone, and you should say so in the pitch. A transparency tool that only works on good hardware isn't a transparency tool.

If you want one ML component for credibility, make it small and honest: a lightweight classifier that guesses camera type from a submitted photo, with a confidence score, human-confirmed before it hits the map. Never auto-trusted. That answers "where's the AI" without pretending.
